I didn't start in software. I started in a field job, driving a route.

It was honest work and I learned a lot from it, but I don't really miss it.

Then I got into software, a little at a time, and I really enjoyed it. I liked sitting with a problem, getting it
wrong a bunch of times, and finally watching it run.

So it feels kind of strange to say this, but I think that part of my job is going away.

## I don't remember deciding to stop reading

I didn't type this site's code. Coding agents wrote it. I look at the site on my phone and tell them what's wrong.

The other day I looked up how much I've been reviewing all of this. I've merged more than 50 pull requests on this
site, and GitHub shows zero reviews and zero inline comments.

I don't remember deciding to stop reading the code. It just kind of happened.

I don't think it's only me, either. When
[Sonar](https://www.sonarsource.com/company/press-releases/sonar-data-reveals-critical-verification-gap-in-ai-coding/),
which sells code quality tools, surveyed over 1,100 developers, 96% said they don't fully trust that AI code is
correct. Only 48% said they always check it before they commit.

[Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/measuring-agent-autonomy), which makes Claude Code, found new users let
it run on full auto-approve in about 20% of sessions. By 750 sessions, it's over 40%. Those same people interrupt it
more, so it looks to me like they aren't checking out. They just stop reading every step and jump in when something
looks off.

And the amount of code keeps climbing. [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/reports/state-of-ai-coding), which sells AI
code review, says lines changed per developer on its customers' teams went from about 6,000 to about 21,000 in a year.
[A study of 26 open source projects](https://dev.to/aictrl/the-abandoned-pr-page-55ad) saw merged pull requests with no
human comment go from 42% to 54% in the established ones. I've seen something like this in my own work too: a lot more
lines shipped to prod, and a lot fewer changes requested by people.

I don't want to read too much into that. Part of it is probably just people getting tired of reviewing.
[DORA's latest report](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report) ties
more AI use to less stable delivery. And every test on this site was green, but on my phone the site was still unusable.

So I still think we should trust but verify.

But I don't think people stop checking something they expect to be wrong most of the time. My guess is they stop when
it's been right enough times. To me, that's a sign it can do our jobs a bit more than we give it credit for.

## It's not just code

I doubt anyone in the back seat of a Waymo is checking the mirrors.

[Waymo says](https://waymo.com/blog/shorts/safetydata-september26/) it's had 95% fewer serious injury crashes than human
drivers over 270 million miles, and that's its own number.
[IIHS](https://iihs.org/news/detail/waymos-driverless-cars-crash-less-often-than-people), which doesn't work for Waymo,
ran its own study and found 68% fewer police-reportable crashes per mile.

Humanoid robots are a lot earlier. [Figure's robots](https://www.figure.ai/news/production-at-bmw) loaded about 90,000
parts at a BMW plant over 11 months, and that's one narrow job. But I'd guess that's how this starts.

When I picture where it goes, I think one day we'll look back at a lot of this work and be like:

> "Yeah, that was kind of a shitty job, and I'm glad nobody has to do that now."

Driving all night to make a delivery window. Lifting boxes until your back gives out.

In 1880, the horses in New York and Brooklyn left
[three to four million pounds of manure a day](https://escholarship.org/content/qt6sm968t2/qt6sm968t2.pdf) on the
streets and in the stables. Somebody had to shovel that. I've never heard anyone say they wish they'd had that job.

## You will be missed

I don't think that's how I'm going to feel about this one.

I also don't want to pretend getting there is easy. I doubt anybody losing a paycheck feels lucky. When dial phones
replaced telephone operators, [the ones already in the job and over 25](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28061) were less
likely to be working ten years later. I still have to provide, and I honestly don't know what that looks like in ten
years.

But someday somebody's going to ask me what programming was like. I'll probably tell them about the 3 a.m. pages and the
bug that took all week. Then I'll tell them about the time it finally worked, and I'll get a little sentimental about it.

So, to writing code by hand: you were good to me.

You will be missed.
